Wearables

Wearables are technology worn on the body — from smart watches and body-mounted sensors, to electronics embedded in clothing, to purely mechanical devices like arm and leg braces. Many CRE[AT]E Challenge projects take the form of a wearable for the co-designer.

Electronic wearables typically involve microprocessors, sensors, a power source (usually lithium-ion), and output devices such as LEDs, screens, motors, haptic feedback, or data analytics.

Hardware

Electronic Wearables — Components

  • Microprocessors — Arduino is popular for prototyping, but Adafruit's Lilypad, Bluefruit, and Feather boards are specifically designed with wearables in mind, offering a smaller form factor and power-conscious design.
  • Sensors — collect data from the user's body or environment.
  • Power Source — most wearables use small rechargeable lithium-ion batteries, so energy efficiency is important.
  • Output Devices — LED lights, touchscreen displays, motors, haptic feedback, or data sent for analytics.

Design Considerations

Key Considerations for Wearables

1. Form Factor & Ergonomics

Minimizing discomfort is critical: think about pressure points, irritation, and strain during use. Also consider donning and doffing — can the user put the device on and take it off independently? Are the movements required appropriate for the user? Simplify where possible.

2. Measuring for Wearables

Bodies are complex and dynamic:

  • Bodies are flexible — measure multiple times, and look up anatomical measurement procedures for consistency.
  • Bodies move — does the wearable's placement flex or shift? Consider elastic or knit fabrics to prevent slipping.
  • Bodies change — children grow, and people in rehabilitation change, so add adjustability for long-term usability.
  • Bodies need hygiene — wearables in extended skin contact must be cleanable, so consider washable covers or sweat/odor-resistant materials.

3. Aesthetics

Wearable products are more visible in the user's daily life than most other AT products. How a product looks can directly affect whether it gets used — especially for children — so consider the co-designer's aesthetic opinions and adjust accordingly.

Partnership

Wearable Technology Curriculum

The CRE[AT]E Challenge partners with the Advanced Functional Fabrics of America (AFFOA) — one of 17 Manufacturing USA institutes — whose mission is to rekindle the domestic textiles industry through advanced fiber and fabric technology.

AFFOA's AFF Engineering and Design with Prototyping Unit Curriculum teaches engineering and design process through actual functional wearable prototypes. Access the curriculum at curriculum.affoa.org/EDP.
