Mechanical

This section covers the physical fabrication skills behind building an assistive technology device — machine tools and shops, 3D printing, and computer-aided design (CAD). Mechanical is one of four Maker Skills sections; if your project involves mechanical fabrication, complete this section along with one other that fits your project.

Fabrication

Machine Tools and Shops

A machine shop is a workshop with tools primarily used for subtractive manufacturing — making products by cutting into materials — such as lathes, mills, drill presses, and laser cutters. Most machine shops now also include tools for additive manufacturing, like 3D printers.

A shop tour from StuffMadeHere, included to give you a sense of what tools are commonly available in a well-equipped machine shop.

A virtual tour of the MIT Beaver Works space is available at my.matterport.com. Take some time to walk through it — find the prototyping shop and see if you can identify some of the tools and safety equipment you spot there.

Activity

For the CRE[AT]E Challenge, teams should also consider what maker tools are available at home, through school, prototyping services, or a local makerspace. Find and describe a makerspace near you, including:

  • Its name and location
  • What tools are available there
  • How to access it (membership, fees, training requirements, etc.)

Additive Manufacturing

3D Printing

3D printing is a collection of methods by which a computer-controlled device creates a three-dimensional object by adding material bit by bit. It is widely used for rapid prototyping of products, and it will likely be one of the most useful tools available to your team.

An introductory video covering the basics of 3D printing.

Comprehension Check

  1. What is the most common type of 3D printing filament?
  2. What is G-code, and how is it usually generated?
  3. What does infill refer to?
  4. What are the ways that model orientation might affect a 3D print?
  5. For an FDM printer, what is another reason to use certain model orientations over others? (Hint: think about a lever printed lying horizontal vs. standing vertical — how does the layering direction affect strength?)

Design Software

Computer-Aided Design (CAD)

BWSI AT's baseline CAD software is Onshape, a web-based CAD program. We recommend Onshape because it is free for academic use, platform-agnostic (it runs entirely in your browser, with no installation required), and built for collaboration, so your whole team can work in the same document at once.

Getting Started with Onshape

  1. Create a free account at onshape.com/products/education.
  2. Complete these Self-Paced Courses in the Onshape Learning Center:
    • Navigating Onshape
    • Introduction to Sketching
    • Part Design Using Part Studios

Other CAD options include Fusion 360 and Tinkercad (both free for students, from Autodesk) — see the Additional Resources page for more.
