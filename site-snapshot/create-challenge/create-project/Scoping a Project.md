# Scoping a Project

Project scope is driven by the co-designer's needs, the team's expertise, and available resources (funding, equipment, mentorship, etc). A good solution that is fully executed beats an amazing solution that is only half-built.

## Team exercise: Taking Stock

Guided reflection to get rough estimates of what the team has to work with, in order to rule out overly ambitious ideas before committing to them. Covers three factors:

- **Time** — meeting frequency/length and whether it will stay constant through the school year; other time commitments (class load, sports, music, home responsibilities).
- **Funding** — how much money is available, which aspects of the design need it, and whether/how fundraising would affect the project timeline.
- **Expertise** — team members' current skills and collective sense of the build process; access to outside experts, including Beaver Works technical mentors for BWSI-supported teams.

Closing guidance: compare these factors against project ideas to see what the team has "in stock" versus what it needs more of, and reconsider an idea (or find more resources) if the team is well short of something it needs.

## What we (generally) don't recommend

Project types flagged as often too ambitious or high-risk, requiring a CRE[AT]E mentor conversation before pursuing:

- exoskeletons (or more broadly, orthotics and body-worn actuators)
- robots (especially more general-purpose robots)
- modifications to wheelchair actuation (e.g. adding motors)

Also disallowed: safety-critical products, defined as products where failure would create an unsafe situation worse than if the product didn't exist (including situations where a user relied on it working). Example given: street-crossing aids for visually impaired users — failure isn't inherently worse than the status quo, but a user expecting the product's help who doesn't get it could be placed in danger.
