What is artificial intelligence?

Artificial intelligence is a notoriously squishy thing to define. Sometimes it is thought of as broadly as simulations of human intelligence by machines (which begs the question of what human intelligence means). Other times, it is about agents that perceive and act on external information — an observe-orient-decide-act (OODA) loop. Still others, it is something we never have because as soon as it exists, it's not sufficiently fancy/esoteric/awe-inspiring to be considered "AI".

The details of the broadest definitions of AI do not matter for this course. However, there are certain things you should know when interacting with AI in this context.

If you interact significantly with AI in this course, it will be one or both of two modes: using AI to help you build your product (AI as tool), or using AI as part of your product (AI as component). The question of whether your team uses AI to assist in the creation of your product is separate from the question of whether AI is part of your product. While the use of AI as a development tool primarily affects the team's learning experience, the inclusion of AI components in your product primarily affect the user of the product — that is, your co-designer. An understanding of AI, and proper responsibility for AI use is crucial.

In both cases, we want you to know some general things about AI, as well as some specific considerations for things like large language models (LLMs) and AI use in the disability space.

This module does not attempt to cover the breadth of AI or its application to disability technology. Instead, it focuses heavily on specific areas of AI, such as generative AI, as well as the related ethical and learning implications of their use in education and in product design for people with disabilities, and was developed based on the experiences of previous years' Beaver Works students and staff.

Beyond Chatbots

AI is not just chatbots

Chatbots like ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, etc are a form of AI, and often one of the most obvious forms of AI that people regularly interact with, but not all AI is chatbots.

Early versions of modern "artificial intelligence" often focused on games like chess, which were seen as a sign of human intelligence. The defeat of chess world champion Garry Kasparov in 1996 by a program run on the Deep Blue supercomputer showed that chess was decided a task that computers could do better than humans. Today, grandmaster-level chess playing programs can be run on as little as a Raspberry Pi 5 or a smartphone.

Other tasks that were gradually shown to be doable by AI included

  • speech recognition
  • optical character recognition (text recognition)
  • image recognition within a given set of labels
  • arbitrary image recognition
  • image captioning
  • photorealistic image generation
  • increasingly complex games, including Atari games, Starcraft 2, DotA2, and Go
  • arbitrary human-like text generation
  • protein design
  • code generation
  • mathematical theorem proving (for theorems unproven by humans)

There is a wide range of tasks that AI is used for, not just chatting!

One other thing to know is that there are different ways to go about building "artificial intelligence". Early AI was simply a set of programmed rules written by a human programmer. A major advance was the introduction of "machine learning" — where the "rules" that the system uses are not directly written, but are learned from examples, and may be represented in a way that is incomprehensible to humans. Machine learning is very helpful for developing systems where the rules for how to complete a task are difficult for a human to accurately and precisely describe (for example, think about describing how to determine whether a set of pixels shows a cat or not).

The diagram above shows overlaps between different types of AI. Note that generative AI isn't necessarily machine learning (but very, very frequently is), and the same goes for chatbots. If you're interested in older examples of generative AI and chatbots, you might be interested in King James Programming and ELIZA.

Neural Networks

Neural networks are a type of machine learning (possibly the most common type these days) where data is used to train a set of weights and biases that are (very roughly) modeled after neurons in the human brain. When many of these neurons are used together in long chains, we call it deep learning. Please watch the following video explaining neural networks.

Capabilities

What can LLMs do?

The capabilities of large language models have increased dramatically over the past decade, particularly since the research preview release of ChatGPT in 2023, which put the idea of human-like AI language generation into everyday experience. In particular, large language models are now often used for natural-language conversations, writing, code generation, information retrieval, and general automation of computer-based tasks.

The biggest shift in work due to LLMs has likely been its impact on software engineering. While it was once only able to act as a slightly smarter auto-complete for code, LLMs are now able to write entire software products when guided by humans with some software experience, and even sometimes by humans with no such experience.

On the educational front, LLMs' ability to produce human-like essays has rendered take-home written work and exams effectively useless as measures of student learning in many areas, particularly in the humanities.

We address some of the broader consequences of these abilities in the section below on educational impacts.

Know Before You Use

The Ethics of Large Language Models

The development and use of large language models involves substantial ethical issues. We want you to be aware of what happened to enable you to "just ask an AI" a question, and what continues to happen when you use these systems, before you decide in your own teams how you will approach their use.

Intellectual Property

Modern large language models require a lot of data to train. Basically any and every piece of human-generated language is data that is of interest. While early machine learning chatbots were trained using open datasets like Reddit and fanfiction websites (producing very "interesting" results at times due to their training data), since the initial release of GPT-3, commercial LLM training has relied heavily on vacuuming up any human-produced text, generally ignoring any copyright or intellectual property laws or policies, and typically using pirated copies of books. Many large content producers such as newspapers have since signed licensing deals with these companies, but only after their materials were already used without consent.

Anthropic, for example, paid $1.5B to settle a copyright infringement case in which they used a database of pirated books to train their models. The same database was used by virtually every large LLM-producing company. At the same time, Anthropic and OpenAI have complained that Chinese companies such as DeepSeek, Moonshot AI, and MiniMax, are using their models for training, without permission.

A large number of lawsuits against virtually every commercial LLM company (OpenAI (ChatGPT), Microsoft (Copilot), Google (Gemini, Gemma), Anthropic (Claude), Meta (LLama, Muse), etc) are ongoing as of 2026 for large-scale intellectual property violations. The companies claim that training their models on copyrighted or otherwise protected information is legal fair use, even if they obtained the materials in violation of law or policy. Similar lawsuits exist for image generators, which are generally trained on any image or art the companies can obtain, regardless of copyright.

You should know that if you produce any intellectual property in this Challenge (or elsewhere) it is virtually guaranteed that it be used to train large language models regardless of whether you know or explicitly consent.

This will likely be before you even make the materials public, especially if you are using the free versions of any software from any of the companies listed above, or any other commercial provider of large language models. It is quite likely that the design reviews you upload to YouTube are being used to train Google's AI. As of 2026, there is currently no opt-out based on the privacy settings of the videos or the fact that you are possibly a minor.

Moderation and Training

AI models almost always perform better the more data they are given. Modern generative AI models take advantage of this fact by using truly massive numbers of parameters, and, as previously described, ingesting as much content as possible. Given that much of the content is from the internet, there is some amount of it that is explicit, harmful, or otherwise inappropriate. LLM producers take varying degrees of safeguarding steps to limit the dangers posed in these ways, but the probabilistic and generative nature of these models means there is no surefire way to prevent general-purpose large language models from producing harmful content.

The work of filtering or reducing the harmful content produced by these models often falls to human data labelers and content moderators, most of whom are located in low-income English-speaking countries. The repeated exposure to some of the worst content produced by people and AI, and having to engage enough with it to judge it along various categories, means that these labeling and moderation workers often suffer severe psychological harms and damage to their relationships.

Recommended resources for the ethics of large language models:

  • Empire of AI by Karen Hao (parental guidance recommended for some content)
  • Magnifica Humanitas by Pope Leo XIV (particularly Chapters 3 and 4)

Learning Impacts

Educational Risks

One of the major challenge of the use of AI in educational contexts like the Challenge is that it simply makes it so that students don't learn very much when they use AI in most cases. In these settings, having a generative AI system produce your work is similar to paying someone to do your homework — sure, it gets done, but what did you get from it? We guarantee you that there are enough answers to the assignments you will be given that the world probably does not need your contribution in the form of a homework submission. The point is not for the assignment to come into existence, but rather for you, the student to put effort into producing it so that you learn from the process.

In 2024, an MIT professor conducted an experiment in his class where he split students up into three groups and had them complete a programming assignment in a language that is fairly uncommon these days, and that none of the students were familiar with. One group was allowed to use ChatGPT, a second group was allowed to use Code Llama (a coding-specific large language model), and the last was only allowed to use Google (this was 2024, so before Google automatically put generative AI in every search result).

The ChatGPT group solved the problem the fastest, the Code Llama group took a bit longer, and the (non-AI-)Google group took longest. Then the professor gave the students a test to see if they understood how they solved the problem. The Google group all passed. About half the Code Llama group passed. Every member of the ChatGPT group failed.

From Eric Klopner, the professor in question, the ChatGPT students "remembered nothing, and they all failed... This is an important educational lesson. Working hard and struggling is actually an important way of learning. When you're given an answer, you're not struggling and you're not learning. And when you get more of a complex problem, it's tedious to go back to the beginning of a large language model and troubleshoot it and integrate it."

A similar study was performed at the MIT Media Lab, this time reading people's brain electrical activity as they worked on a series of essays with and without the use of ChatGPT.

"The group that wrote essays using ChatGPT all delivered extremely similar essays that lacked original thought, relying on the same expressions and ideas... [The brain-only] group was more engaged and curious, and claimed ownership and expressed higher satisfaction with their essays." A third group that used Google search was similar to the brain-only group.

Since 2024, LLMs have advanced even more, and the range of tasks they can complete has expanded.

In the 2025-2026 iteration of the CRE[AT]E Challenge, one team (that will remain anonymous) used a large language model to help them build an app. They misidentified the language that the app was written in, thinking it was Java (it was HTML and JavaScript). They struggled to fix problems with their app or even asking for help with it, because they were initially unfamiliar with how the app was built. Three of the team members were in their second year of AP Computer Science.

There is a difference between the use of generative AI in tasks where the user is already very knowledgeable vs tasks where the user is still learning the task. Experts are typically much more able to effectively use generative AI because they know what to look for, they know how to "coach" the system, and how to check outputs. Learners, on the other hand, often get lost.

It is extremely likely that greater use of generative AI in your education will cause your learning to suffer.

In the CRE[AT]E Challenge, we are not surprised that you can lift more if you use a forklift at the gym. We're just a little confused about why you're at the gym.

On the other hand, you may well be able to produce a product you could not otherwise have put together, but one that may be fragile because your team may not be able to identify or fix problems with it. How to manage some of these risks will be part of your assignment for this module later on.

Note on "Googling":

As of mid-2026, Google search prominently features AI summaries or answers rather than actual search results as a priority. To have an approach that replicates some of the findings above where students engaged with actual search results requires the use of the "-ai" tag in Google. For example, for results on Alzheimer's disease without AI summaries, you need to search "Alzheimer's disease -ai". Note that this does not prevent Google from returning webpages that are themselves AI-generated, and tends to produce more meaningful differences when you are searching for procedural information, such as how to program a specific kind of algorithm.

Alternatively, consider using a search engine like DuckDuckGo that allows you to turn off AI mode.

Where the Model Lives

Privacy and Consent

Apart from the ethical issues that come from training many contemporary AI models, the ecosystem in which these systems often operate can present varying levels of risks in terms of privacy and consent. One key question to ask is where does your AI model live? Whether it is an image recognition system, a biometric tracker, or a chatbot, the model that drives these interactions has to actually exist somewhere. Generally, it is either "in the cloud" or "local".

To be clear, the "cloud" is also just a computer — just not yours. Computation does not happen for free. Generally speaking, if you are not paying for a product, your data is the product. That is, if your AI is running on somebody else's computer and you aren't paying for it, that's because the data that is flowing through the system is being collected and used for training, for ad targeting, and possibly sold. Sometimes, paying for a subscription means that your data will not be used in this way, but that is subject to the specific terms of the subscriptions. While there are some guardrails that exist on this kind of data harvesting, particularly in the European Union, as a general rule, the legal landscape is much slower to adapt commercial interests and technological capabilities.

Consider what this means for AI models being used in your products. If you are designing a product that is to be used by a thirteen year old with severe autism, and it has a permanent connection to OpenAI through ChatGPT, is the user able to consent to their data being collected and transmitted in this way? (This is very similar to a previous Challenge project.) What are the risks if the product is help evaluate and decide on clothing choices (a product category another previous Challenge project has pursued)? Could the user accidentally upload unclothed images of himself that then sit in OpenAI's servers forever? What about if the product is designed as a social companion? Might the user treat it as a "friend" and provide deeply personal information?

It is possible to use AI on computers that you do own, and that is usually referred to as "local" or "edge" computing ("edge" often referring to lower-power devices than standard personal computers, such as microcontrollers). In those cases, models must be downloaded on your own systems, and data can stay within your control. This kind of setup often (but not always) requires some more technical know-how than with cloud systems. In these cases, the models you are able to run are heavily dependent on the capabilities of your own hardware, and will effectively always be less powerful and much slower, but depending on your application, this does not necessarily affect your product's overall performance.

Local AI Models

Examples of local models and related infrastructure include the following:

  • Large language model interfaces: llama.cpp, llamafile, ollama
  • Large language models: Gemma 4 family, Llama 4 family, Phi-4 family
  • Object detection: DETR, YOLO26
  • Automatic speech recognition: Nemotron 3.5, Whisper v3

HuggingFace hosts a large repository of open models and datasets. Note that the examples above have highly variable hardware requirements and use cases, and may or may not work for you. Many models have variants designed for different levels of computational power and use cases, so it is worth exploring to see what might suit your needs.

Designing With Your Co-Designer

AI and Disability

Remember that much of modern AI falls within machine learning, which is developed through the use of data. The question then, is where does that data come from, and is it representative of the experiences that people with disabilities may have?

One way to think about this problem is where the input of the AI comes from during use — is the input something produced by the co-designer (particularly something affected by a disability) or the input something from the external world?

For example, if the system is taking input from the movements of someone with spasticity (tightened muscles due to a neurological condition), or from speech by someone with atypical vocal patterns, then typical machine learning models are unlikely to perform well. It may be possible to adapt the models and "fine-tune" them to work for that person, but that is something to be considered. Several CRE[AT]E projects in the past have taken this approach, and gathering, cleaning, and using data from individuals with disabilities to make these models work were a significant part of their efforts.

On the other hand, if the input to the AI comes from the outside world and it is only the output of the model that is being used by the person with a disability, then typical machine learning models may indeed work as claimed. A scene description AI works as well for a blind person as it does for a sighted person... though you may also consider that a blind person may not normally have the lights on at home, making the model perform poorly in the dark at night!

There are many ways in which AI can be used to help people with disabilities, but we should be careful to consider whether the AI we are using matches the experiences of the user, or if we need to make adjustments.

AI as a Tool

Your Team's Approach to AI as a Tool

Most other Beaver Works courses have instructor-provided guidelines or policies about AI use, particularly for programs where the focus of the learning involves coding. The CRE[AT]E Challenge is unusual in that it is not fundamentally a computer science or coding course, but rather a design course. We will therefore offer just three rules, and let you and your team decide on many of the specifics beyond those, taking into consideration what you have learned in this section.

What we require:

  • The use of large language models and image generation tools for course submissions or project documentation is strictly prohibited.
  • Your team is responsible for reading, understanding, and following the policies and restrictions imposed by AI providers for the use of these products.
  • The use of large language models and image generation tools to help you write code or create other elements of your project is subject to your team's AI use policy (below), and must be disclosed and explained.

Course submissions and project documentation are the core learning-related deliverables for the Challenge, so having AI do your work here is like having someone else do your homework. In contrast, the reason we allow you to decide how you want to use AI tools to create other parts of the project is because these tools are very much part of the tools used in contemporary production, though there is the tradeoff that the more you use them, the less you learn about the parts of the project that are being done for you. If you do choose to use AI tools in your team, we encourage you to be very strategic about when and how you use them as to not take away from your learning, and try to use them to maximize your learning in areas where you aren't using them.

Assignment

Assignment: Draft an AI Tool Use Policy

If your team intends to use AI as a tool in your product development, you must collaboratively produce the following:

  • A single set of AI use rules.
  • An explanation for your choices above, considering the benefits and risks of your decisions. Think about the impacts on your learning and your project, as well as any ethical concerns that come about because of them.
  • The names of all student team members at the bottom, which will be considered your signatures for this document.

Feel free to do additional research before you make your decision. We expect that these policies will focus on generative AI and data storage and transmission, but feel free to adjust as appropriate.

You are allowed to go as far as to decide to entirely ban the use of AI in your team, or to say that you have no additional policies other than the course-mandated documentation requirement above. You must still justify your decision.

Your team's AI use policy will be considered when staff are examining your work. Teams that are heavily using AI as tools will be expected to perform more work on the co-designer interaction and product testing components, since they will likely not spend nearly as much time building the products themselves. Additionally, staff and mentors may attempt to help you understand your code, but if it is AI-generated and you have very little understanding of the code yourself, staff and mentor help will be extremely limited. It is very difficult to talk you through a problem that you created but do not understand.

You may change your team's AI Tool Use Policy during the season if you wish, but in order to do so, you must email CRE[AT]E Challenge staff with all team members and coaches cc'd with an explanation of and justification for the change.

AI as a Component

Your Team's Approach to AI as a Component of Your Project

Many assistive technology products are deeply personal items. That means that very serious consideration must be given to the protection of user privacy and the respect for consent. This is particularly true in the cases of projects done in this Challenge, many of which are for people who are unable to legally provide consent — for example, minors, or people with cognitive disabilities. The items below apply to commercial, cloud-based AI systems, and not to local AI models. As a general rule, if you can only run the model with an internet connection, it is a cloud-based system. You can test this very easily yourself.

What we require:

  • Products intended for users who cannot legally provide consent shall not use commercial, cloud-based AI systems.
  • Teams using commercial, cloud-based AI systems for users who are able to legally consent, must explain to the user(s), to the extent possible, what models are being used, what happens to their data, and who owns the data, and the users must agree to these terms.
  • For high school teams, the adult coach must be involved in this process. If the team or coach cannot explain these items to the user, the cloud-based system shall not be used.

Previous iterations of this Challenge included examples of products that violated the principles above. The space is developing quickly, and the risks are still being understood. As previously mentioned, the legal and policy landscape moves much more slowly than the technological capabilities in this space.

Please note that being a minor (under 18) or having a developmental disability are two of the most common user categories that would trigger the no-cloud requirement. "Commercial cloud-based" AI systems include anything that you need an internet connection to run, regardless of whether you need an account or not.

Assignment

Assignment: Draft an AI Component Policy

If your team is considering the use of AI (defined broadly) as a component in your product, in addition to the rules above, you must collaboratively produce the following:

  • A list of consent, privacy, safety, and ethical risks for the use of AI in your product.
  • A list of mitigations for these risks.
  • A description of how and what you will communicate of the above to your co-designer(s) and address concerns (including the exclusion of AI components). Keep in mind that your communication must consider what your co-designer understands of the explanation.

The use of local models is a broad mitigation for many of the consent and privacy concerns that might arise, but even in these cases, you must still consider what other safety and ethical concerns exist for the user. For example, the Challenge has always disallowed the development of safety-critical projects, such as computer vision cameras that help visually impaired people cross the street (if they fail, injury or death is a reasonable risk). With large language models and their use in everyday life, you must now also consider other factors, such as impacts on social and emotional well-being for social products, or medical or financial risks for tools in those domains.

We also encourage you to consider whether generative AI in particular is required at all for your product, as opposed to some other kind of AI, as it tends to be less predictable and have greater risks of harm. A previous team, for example, had used a large language model to provide word definitions in an educational support product before realizing that they could just connect the product to a dictionary database.
